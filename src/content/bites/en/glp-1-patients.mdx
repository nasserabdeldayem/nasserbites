## How does the medicine work?

After you eat, your gut releases a natural hormone called **GLP-1**. The medicine copies this hormone but stays in the body much longer, and it works in four places:

<Cards>
  <Card title="Brain">Sends a fullness signal, so you feel less hungry and think about food less.</Card>
  <Card title="Stomach">Slows down how fast food leaves the stomach, so you stay full for longer.</Card>
  <Card title="Pancreas">Raises insulin only when blood sugar is high, so it rarely causes low blood sugar when used on its own.</Card>
  <Card title="Liver">Lowers the hormone glucagon, so the liver releases less sugar into the blood.</Card>
</Cards>

<Callout type="result">

**The result:** better-controlled blood sugar, lower weight, and protection for the heart and kidneys in certain groups of patients.

</Callout>

## Common side effects

They usually appear in the first few weeks or after a dose increase, and tend to ease with time.

<Chips items={["Nausea", "Diarrhoea", "Constipation", "Vomiting", "Heartburn and burping", "Bloating", "Loss of appetite", "Redness at the injection site"]} />

<Callout type="tips" title="Tips that ease these symptoms">

- Eat small meals, and eat slowly
- Cut down on fatty food, fried food and sweets
- Don't lie down right after eating
- Stop at the first feeling of fullness
- Drink enough water, especially in hot weather
- Don't increase your dose before it's due

</Callout>

<Callout type="note">

If you also take **insulin** or a **sulfonylurea** (such as gliclazide), your blood sugar may drop too low. Ask your doctor about adjusting the dose.

</Callout>

<Callout type="danger" title="Get medical help straight away if you have:">

- Severe, ongoing stomach pain that may spread to your back
- Pain in the upper right of your abdomen, a fever, or yellowing of your eyes
- Vomiting or diarrhoea that won't stop, and you can't keep fluids down
- Sudden loss of vision in one eye
- A lump in your neck, trouble swallowing, or a hoarse voice that doesn't go away

</Callout>

## Protect your muscles and health during treatment

Because you'll eat less on these medicines, your body needs extra care in three areas: **protein**, **water**, and **vitamins and minerals**.

### Protein: to protect your muscles

When weight comes off quickly, the body loses not only fat but also some **muscle**. Losing muscle weakens your strength and balance and slows down how much energy you burn, especially as you get older. The best protection is enough protein plus strength exercise.

<Figure src="/images/glp-1/protein-foods.jpg" alt="Raw chicken breast, eggs, almonds and lentils on a wooden board" caption="Simple protein sources: chicken, eggs, lentils and almonds" credit="Smastronardo" creditHref="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Protein-rich_Foods.jpg" license="CC BY-SA 4.0" licenseHref="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/" />

<Callout type="tips" title="How to get enough protein">

- Aim for 20 to 30 grams of protein at each main meal
- Eat the protein on your plate first, before starches, because you fill up quickly
- If eating is hard, a protein shake can help; ask your pharmacist which one suits you
- Do strength exercise (weights, resistance bands, or bodyweight exercises) 2–3 times a week

</Callout>

Roughly how much protein is in:

<Chips items={["1 egg: 6 g", "1 pot of Greek yoghurt: 15 g", "100 g cooked chicken breast: 30 g", "100 g fish or tuna: 20–25 g", "1 cup cooked lentils: 18 g", "100 g cottage cheese: 11 g"]} />

<Callout type="note">

If you have **chronic kidney disease**, don't increase your protein until your doctor tells you how much is right for you.

</Callout>

### Water: because you may not feel thirsty

These medicines reduce appetite and sometimes thirst, and vomiting or diarrhoea make you lose extra fluid. Dehydration can strain your kidneys, so drink even when you're not thirsty.

<Callout type="tips" title="Hydration tips">

- Sip small amounts through the day instead of a lot at once
- Aim for about 8 glasses (2 litres) a day, unless your doctor has given you a different amount (for example with heart or kidney disease)
- Drink more in hot weather and when you exercise
- Pale urine is a good sign; dark urine means you need more water
- If you're vomiting or have diarrhoea, use oral rehydration salts from the pharmacy
- Cut down on fizzy drinks, as they make bloating worse

</Callout>

### Vitamins and minerals: because you're eating less

When you eat less, your body also gets fewer vitamins and minerals. So:

<Figure src="/images/glp-1/calcium-vitamin-d.jpg" alt="White calcium and vitamin D supplement tablets on a black background" caption="Calcium and vitamin D supplements: only start them after advice" credit="Ragesoss" creditHref="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:500_mg_calcium_supplements_with_vitamin_D.jpg" license="CC BY-SA 4.0" licenseHref="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/" />

- **Vary your food:** vegetables and fruit of different colours, whole grains, and beans or lentils. Fibre also helps ease constipation.
- **Watch these nutrients:** vitamin D and calcium for your bones, iron, and vitamin B12 (especially if you also take **metformin**, which can lower B12 absorption).
- **Ask your doctor or pharmacist** whether you need a daily multivitamin or regular blood tests, and don't start high-dose supplements without advice.
