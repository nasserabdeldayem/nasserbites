## A disease of dark cities

In the 17th century, a disease spread among children in European cities that made bones soft, so legs bowed and walking came late: **rickets**. The English physician Francis Glisson described it in detail in 1650, and with the Industrial Revolution it became very common in crowded cities where smoke blocked out the sun.

## Two cures, no explanation

People noticed two things that cured rickets:

- **Cod liver oil:** an old folk remedy in northern Europe.
- **Sunlight:** in 1822, the Polish physician Jędrzej Śniadecki noticed that children in Warsaw got rickets far more often than children in the countryside, and advised getting them out into the sun.

But nobody knew why two completely different cures worked the same way.

## The years of discovery

<Cards>
  <Card title="1919 · Berlin">Kurt Huldschinsky treated children with rickets using ultraviolet lamps, and showed with X-rays that their bones had healed.</Card>
  <Card title="1919 · London">Edward Mellanby gave puppies rickets by keeping them indoors on a limited diet, then cured them with cod liver oil. He thought the secret was vitamin A.</Card>
  <Card title="1922 · Baltimore">Elmer McCollum heated cod liver oil until its vitamin A was destroyed, and the oil still cured rickets. So there was a new substance, which he named **vitamin D**.</Card>
  <Card title="1924 · Wisconsin">Harry Steenbock found that shining ultraviolet light on food gave it the same effect. This led to milk being fortified with vitamin D, and rickets almost disappeared in many countries.</Card>
</Cards>

## A vitamin the body makes itself

The answer to the puzzle was simple: **the skin makes vitamin D itself** when ultraviolet (UVB) sunlight reaches it, and cod liver oil contains it ready-made. In the 1930s, the German chemist Adolf Windaus worked out its chemical structure, after winning the 1928 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his research on sterols and their link to vitamins.

## From vitamin to hormone

In the 1960s and 1970s, Hector DeLuca and other research teams discovered that vitamin D doesn't work as it is. It passes through four stops:

<Cards>
  <Card title="1. Skin">In sunlight, vitamin D3 is made in the skin from a cholesterol-like substance, or it comes ready-made from food and supplements.</Card>
  <Card title="2. Liver">Turns it into **25-hydroxyvitamin D**, the stored form that a blood test measures.</Card>
  <Card title="3. Kidneys">Turn it into the active form: **calcitriol** (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D).</Card>
  <Card title="4. Whole body">It binds to receptors in the gut, bones and many other tissues, raising the absorption of calcium and phosphate and strengthening bones.</Card>
</Cards>

<Callout type="result">

**This is why vitamin D counts as a hormone:** the body makes it in one organ, activates it in others, then it travels in the blood to act on receptors in distant tissues, and other hormones, such as parathyroid hormone, control how much is made.

</Callout>

## A pharmacist's angle

<Callout type="tips" title="What this means for you">

- **The blood test** measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, because it reflects the body's stores
- **People with chronic kidney disease** may not be able to activate it, so doctors sometimes prescribe the active form directly (calcitriol or alfacalcidol)
- **Take it with a meal that contains some fat**, because it's a fat-soluble vitamin and is absorbed better that way
- **More isn't better:** high doses over long periods can raise calcium in the blood, so stick to the dose your doctor or pharmacist recommends

</Callout>
