## A hormone from the gut

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) is an **incretin**, a hormone released by L-cells in the lower small intestine and colon when food arrives. Its job is to tell the rest of the body that a meal is on the way.

It does that in a few places at once:

- **Pancreas** — boosts insulin release and lowers glucagon, but *only when glucose is high*
- **Stomach** — slows gastric emptying, so glucose enters the blood more gradually
- **Brain** — acts on appetite centres to increase the feeling of fullness

## The two-minute problem

Natural GLP-1 is broken down by the enzyme **DPP-4** within about two minutes. That is fine for a signal after a meal, but useless as a medicine.

So there were two ways to use this system:

1. **Block the enzyme**: DPP-4 inhibitors like sitagliptin let your own GLP-1 last a little longer.
2. **Build a tougher copy**: GLP-1 receptor agonists resist DPP-4 and stay in the body far longer.

> Exenatide, the first GLP-1 agonist, came from the saliva of the Gila monster, a lizard whose version of the hormone happens to resist DPP-4.

Later drugs were engineered to last longer still. Liraglutide is given once daily, and semaglutide and dulaglutide once weekly.

## Why "glucose-dependent" matters

Because GLP-1 only boosts insulin when glucose is raised, these medicines carry a low risk of hypoglycaemia on their own. That risk goes up when they're combined with insulin or a sulfonylurea, where doses often need adjusting.

## Why nausea is so common

Slower gastric emptying and signals to the brain's satiety centres are part of how these drugs work, so nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea are their most common side effects. This is why doses are increased gradually. A bit like the [GI effects of metformin](/bites/metformin), the side effect is closely tied to the mechanism.
